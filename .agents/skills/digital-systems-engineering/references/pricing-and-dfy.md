# Pricing, DFY, and Certification

Covers DSE's economics: entry pricing, the D'Famocious Year (DFY) revenue-share obligation, and the certification a beneficiary earns on completion. This is real financial structure, not something settled by outside precedent the way SUC's valuation content was — treat the specifics as DBI's own current policy, and flag anything a student asks about that isn't covered here rather than guessing at it.

## Entry pricing

- **DSE, regular:** $900 one-time.
- **DSE, via BEF's NBO (Next Blazers Organisation) subsidy:** $90. NBO is an initiative of BEF (Blazer Empowerment Foundation), D'Famocious Group's NGO subsidiary, built specifically to make DBI's entry point affordable for students who couldn't otherwise access it. NBO participants still pay the standard $2,000 SUC entrance fee later, at the regular rate, since by that point they're already earners.
- **SUC, via the DSE path:** $2,000 — but only if invited (see below for what triggers an invitation). DBI's standard 25% equity applies.
- **SUC, skipping DSE entirely, standard equity:** $6,000, same 25% equity as the DSE path — paying to skip the proof DSE would have generated, not to change the equity terms.
- **SUC, skipping DSE entirely, reduced equity:** $24,000, DBI's stake reduced to 10%. This replaced an earlier no-equity option priced at $18,000-$60,000 across several rounds of revision — worth recording why, not just the final number:
  - **A ladder beats a cliff.** A binary "pay $X for zero DBI equity" option means DBI has no stake at all in that tier's businesses — a structural misalignment a graduated ladder avoids. At every tier here, DBI keeps a real, if shrinking, interest in the business succeeding.
  - **The marginal price should make sense to a confident founder, not just the average one.** The trade at this tier is $18,000 to keep 15 additional percentage points of equity (25% → 10%). If a business's realistic expected value lands anywhere in the low-to-mid hundreds of thousands, 15 points is worth more than $18,000 to someone who genuinely believes in what they're building — which is the right test for a price like this, not whether it looks reasonable against a best-case, business-worth-millions scenario.
  - **No clean external market comparable exists for this specific question.** Founder share buybacks, vesting-based buyback clauses, and early-employee equity purchases are all real, documented practices — but they all price equity in an *already-valued* company. Pricing a stake in a business that doesn't exist yet, at the moment of entry, is a different and genuinely fuzzier problem; the reasoning above is the best available logic, not a benchmarked market rate.

## SUC readiness is outcome-based, not time-based

DSE's real qualification criterion is $450,000 in cumulative earnings within 3-5 years — not simply "years elapsed." A student can be strong on effort and still not be SUC-ready if the earnings threshold isn't met in that window; the timeline is a boundary, not a target. This corrects language used elsewhere (including in the `startup-catalyst` skill) that describes advancement as purely time-based — flag that inconsistency if it comes up, since SUC's own file hasn't yet been updated to match.

## The equivalent-qualification calculator — three tiers, one clean formula

$450,000 in exactly 3-5 years isn't the only path. Genuinely strong performers who need more time than the standard window still qualify, on a real, calculated equivalent — not an exception granted case by case.

**Tier 1 — Performance Per Excellence (years 1-4):** a flat $450,000, regardless of whether it lands in year 3 or year 4. The bar doesn't shrink for taking marginally longer within this window — speed itself is what earns the "excellence" label, not a sliding-scale target.

**Tier 2 — Normal Gate (year 5):** the standard $450,000 threshold. This is the rate everything else is calibrated against: $450,000 ÷ 5 years = **$90,000/year**.

**Tier 3 — Equivalent Qualification (year 5 onward, continuous):** required cumulative earnings = **$90,000 × years elapsed.** Not a jump at year 5 and then a different rule after — the same $90,000/year rate extended smoothly, so it reproduces the normal gate exactly at year 5 and keeps extending indefinitely for anyone who needs more time.

**Worked examples, verified against the rate above:**
- 5 years → $450,000 (the normal gate itself)
- 6 years → $540,000
- 9 years → $810,000
- Any year *T* ≥ 5 → $90,000 × *T*

The door stays open indefinitely in principle — someone taking 12 years at a genuinely sustained $90,000/year pace ($1,080,000 cumulative) qualifies exactly as validly as someone who hit the 5-year normal gate. What's being measured is sustained pace, not speed for its own sake past year 4.

## Trackable from day one, not just a pass/fail check years later

The calculator should be usable from a beneficiary's very first month, not just consulted once 3-5 years have passed. At any point, **pace percentage = (cumulative earnings to date) ÷ ($90,000 × years elapsed) × 100.**

This works as a real-time signal at any stage: within years 1-4, running at or above 100% of this pace indicates a beneficiary is genuinely tracking toward the Excellence tier's flat $450,000, even though the actual qualifying rule in that window is the flat bar, not the prorated percentage itself — the percentage is the useful early-warning signal, not the qualifying test. Past year 5, the same percentage *is* the qualifying test directly, since Tier 3's target is defined continuously by this same rate.

**A beneficiary running below 100% pace isn't failing — the system already has an answer for them:** the Tier 3 formula keeps extending the target the longer they take, at the same rate the whole system was calibrated on from the start. Show all three tiers together where practical, rather than a single number — a beneficiary genuinely may not know yet whether they'll land in Excellence, Normal, or an extended timeline, and seeing their current trajectory against all three is more useful than forcing a single verdict before it's actually knowable.

## DFY: the D'Famocious Year

Once a beneficiary starts earning, 20% of earnings goes back to DBI, for the duration of one qualified year — or until the $18,000 cap is reached, whichever comes first.

- **A qualified month** is any month, counted from when earning began, where net income (before the 20% cut) is at least $2,000. Each qualifying period runs its own BPS Cadence cycle (`dfg`'s `references/bps-cadence.md`) — a Belief Goal roughly 30 days out committing to a specific income-generating plan, an Affirmation Goal at the midpoint checking effort against it, and an Evaluation Goal at the end. Qualification isn't just a pass/fail check against $2,000 at month's end; it's the outcome of a structured, tracked pursuit throughout.
- **A qualified year** is 12 qualified months — not necessarily consecutive. A slow month doesn't reset progress; it simply doesn't count toward the 12.
- **The cap:** total DFY remittance is capped at **$18,000**. Reaching the cap concludes DFY immediately, even with fewer than 12 qualified months completed. Modeled against realistic earning ranges across DSE's seven systems, a solid multi-client performer's total DFY lands well under this cap (roughly $3,600–$14,400 over a genuine qualified year) and never touches it; only an exceptional earner reaches $18,000 early. That's the intended effect — the cap protects against open-ended extraction from the strongest performers without changing the obligation for anyone else, and reaching it early is itself a demonstration of the earning capacity DFY exists to build, not an exemption from the goal.
- **Completion:** DFY concludes at 12 qualified months *or* the $18,000 cap, whichever comes first, and either way the beneficiary receives the **Vetted Digital Systems Engineer (V. DsE.)** certification — a newly created credential, not a pre-existing industry standard.

**The certification isn't permanent by default.** Worth carrying forward from the original earning framework: post-graduation evidence of material ethical breach or financial fraud that compromises the integrity of the original DFY achievement is legitimate grounds for revocation, through the same governance process as any other Level 4 matter. Not something to advertise heavily to beneficiaries, but real, and worth having on record rather than assuming certification is unconditional once granted.

**Graduates aren't simply done — a real Alumni/Mentorship tier now exists**, distinct from but complementary to Leadership by Influence's SUC-eligibility mentorship requirement (`references/leadership-by-influence.md`). The original framing calls this a "multiplying system of empowerment" — graduates retain a relationship of contribution back to the ecosystem, not just a credential and an exit. Its concrete expression: the D'Famocious Annual Honours (`references/annual-recognition.md`) — recognition and real platform for sustained mentorship and SUC-stage achievement, not just the baseline threshold.

**Confirmed, not flagged: there is no off-platform earnings provision.** The original constitution's Personal Buyer Exemption Protocol (a carve-out for earnings routed outside the institutional platform) has been explicitly discontinued. Every dollar earned is managed through the DFY system until either 12 qualified months or the $18,000 cap is reached — no exception, no separate record-keeping-only category.

**How reporting actually works right now, at current (very early) scale:** with the first cohort just starting to earn — a handful of people — the process is manual: a beneficiary reports earnings to their Rep using an accountability sheet listing each buyer and what they paid, by day/week/month, then sends the corresponding DFY remittance to a designated account. This is the live process today, not a hypothetical one. **Planned, not yet built:** a systemized payment platform (Stripe or similar) to automate reporting and remittance as volume grows. Describe the current process accurately when it's relevant, and don't describe the future system as already operational.

**Worked example:** a beneficiary earns $2,400 in month 1 (qualified — 1 of 12, owes 20% = $480, running total $480), $1,600 in month 2 (below threshold — doesn't advance the count, but still owes 20% = $320, running total $800), $3,100 in month 3 (qualified — 2 of 12, owes 20% = $620, running total $1,420), and so on. Every earning month generates a remittance regardless of qualification status; only qualified months move the counter toward 12 — and the running total is checked against the $18,000 cap every month.

**Why it's built this way, not just how:** a sub-threshold month costs real money and doesn't buy any progress toward finishing DFY — there's no such thing as a "free pass" or a coasting month. That's deliberate: it builds urgency and consistency pressure into the DSE period specifically because those are the same muscles SUC demands later. A beneficiary who learns to avoid slow months here is being prepared for SUC, not just taxed during DSE.

## Disclose this before DFY starts, not after

DFY is a real financial obligation and should be presented as one — plainly, before a beneficiary starts earning, not discovered mid-obligation. Never describe DFY as "not a loan" or "not debt," and never use "risk-free" or "no strings attached" language — say plainly what it is.

**The canonical disclosure statement — use this exact language, don't paraphrase it into something softer:**

> "DFY is a revenue-share obligation — 20% of your earnings while it's active, capped at $18,000 total, ending at 12 qualified months or the cap, whichever comes first. This is a real, binding financial commitment; plan for it the way you'd plan for any other significant obligation."

Beyond this statement, full disclosure should also cover: a realistic illustrative range of what DFY could total (roughly $3,600–$14,400 for a solid performer, capped at $18,000 regardless of how strong the year is); that sub-threshold months still owe the 20% even though they don't count toward completion; and where the effective-cost figure below comes from and what it does and doesn't mean.

## The effective-cost figure — lead with scenarios, not a bare APR

A computed implied APR for DFY runs extremely high — roughly 385% to 2,400% across realistic scenarios (solid to exceptional performer), computed by treating the $900 entry fee as the amount financed against the resulting DFY payment stream. That number is technically real but structurally misleading if published alone: it's high not because DFY is unusually expensive, but because the $900 entry fee is small relative to the capped payment stream, unlike a bootcamp ISA where "amount financed" represents real deferred value (tuition) comparable in scale to total payments. Industry guidance is explicit on this point: don't compare ISA-like terms to loan terms unless an effective APR is provided *with* proper context — a bare percentage invites exactly the kind of misunderstanding honest disclosure exists to prevent.

**What to actually lead with:** the scenario table (illustrative total cost at different income levels — already above) as the primary disclosure format, since it's what a beneficiary can actually use to plan. The computed APR range can be included as a secondary, explicitly-caveated figure — state the number, then immediately explain why it reads high, using the entry-fee-size explanation above. Never state the APR without that explanation attached.

## During DFY, the relationship is investor-support, not employer-discipline

This follows from `dfg`'s framing: DFG is a talent pipeline developing an asset, not an employer disciplining a worker, and DFY is the period where that posture matters most, since it's also where the strongest misclassification signal would otherwise live.

**What this means concretely:** a beneficiary with several non-qualified months in a row should trigger a support response, not a disciplinary one — additional coaching, a niche or system reassessment with their Rep, or an honest conversation about whether the current system is the right fit. It should never trigger an escalating sanction. Whatever discipline framework exists elsewhere in DSE (see `nbo`'s `references/training-governance-roles.md` for the parallel NBC context) should not extend into the DFY period in its punitive form — build earning power, don't punish its absence.

**This isn't just a design choice — it's grounded in BEF's own constitution.** Before any discipline ladder applies at all, a prior classification question decides whether it applies: acceptable failure (good-faith effort, external factors) gets review and support with no sanctions; correctable failure (weak systems, a skills gap) gets retraining and an improvement plan; only genuine disciplinary failure (persistent insubordination, real financial mismanagement) reaches formal sanctions. A slow DFY stretch is presumptively the first or second category, not automatically the third — see `bef`'s `references/identity-and-framework.md` for the full classification.

**DFY participants operate under a Code of Honour** — a named, elevated ethical standard distinct from general discipline: strict financial disclosure, no circumventing institutional oversight, and conduct befiting an institutionally-vetted, high-trust economic actor. Frame it to beneficiaries as a mark of trust extended, not an additional set of rules imposed.

## What this structure resembles, worth having on record

DFY's shape — income-contingent payment, triggered only once a real earnings threshold is crossed — closely resembles a real, named financial instrument: an income share agreement (ISA). ISAs draw real regulatory attention in some jurisdictions (effective-APR disclosure, payment caps, treatment under consumer lending law) precisely because they look informal but function as a real financial obligation. That's not a reason to avoid the structure — it's a reason to get the same kind of real legal/financial review here that the SUC ownership policy already got, before this touches a real student's real payments.
