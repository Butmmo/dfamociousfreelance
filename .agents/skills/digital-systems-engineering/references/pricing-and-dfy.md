# Pricing, DFY, and Certification

Covers DSE's economics: entry pricing, the D'Famocious Year (DFY) revenue-share obligation, and the certification a beneficiary earns on completion. This is real financial structure, not something settled by outside precedent the way SUC's valuation content was — treat the specifics as DBI's own current policy, and flag anything a student asks about that isn't covered here rather than guessing at it.

## Entry pricing

- **DSE, regular:** $900 one-time.
- **DSE, via BEF's NBO (Next Blazers Organisation) subsidy:** $90. NBO is an initiative of BEF (Blazer Empowerment Foundation), D'Famocious Group's NGO subsidiary, built specifically to make DBI's entry point affordable for students who couldn't otherwise access it. NBO participants still pay the standard $2,000 SUC entrance fee later, at the regular rate, since by that point they're already earners.
- **SUC, via the DSE path:** $2,000 — but only if invited (see below for what triggers an invitation). DBI's standard 25% equity applies.
- **SUC, skipping DSE entirely, standard equity:** $6,000, same 25% equity as the DSE path — paying to skip the proof DSE would have generated, not to change the equity terms.
- **SUC, skipping DSE entirely, reduced equity:** $24,000, DBI's stake reduced to 10%. This replaced an earlier no-equity option priced at $18,000-$60,000 across several rounds of revision — worth recording why, not just the final number:
  - **A ladder beats a cliff.** A binary "pay $X for zero DBI equity" option means DBI has no stake at all in that tier's businesses — a structural misalignment a graduated ladder avoids. At every tier here, DBI keeps a real, if shrinking, interest in the business succeeding.
  - **The marginal price should make sense to a confident founder, not just the average one.** The trade at this tier is $18,000 to keep 15 additional percentage points of equity (25% → 10%). If a business's realistic expected value lands anywhere in the low-to-mid hundreds of thousands, 15 points is worth more than $18,000 to someone who genuinely believes in what they're building — which is the right test for a price like this, not whether it looks reasonable against a best-case, business-worth-millions scenario.
  - **No clean external market comparable exists for this specific question.** Founder share buybacks, vesting-based buyback clauses, and early-employee equity purchases are all real, documented practices — but they all price equity in an *already-valued* company. Pricing a stake in a business that doesn't exist yet, at the moment of entry, is a different and genuinely fuzzier problem; the reasoning above is the best available logic, not a benchmarked market rate.

## SUC readiness is outcome-based, not time-based

DSE's real qualification criterion is $450,000 in cumulative earnings within 3-5 years — not simply "years elapsed." A student can be strong on effort and still not be SUC-ready if the earnings threshold isn't met in that window; the timeline is a boundary, not a target. This corrects language used elsewhere (including in the `startup-catalyst` skill) that describes advancement as purely time-based — flag that inconsistency if it comes up, since SUC's own file hasn't yet been updated to match.

## DFY: the D'Famocious Year

Once a beneficiary starts earning, 20% of earnings goes back to DBI for the duration of one qualified year.

- **A qualified month** is any month, counted from when earning began, where net income (before the 20% cut) is at least $2,000.
- **A qualified year** is 12 qualified months — not necessarily consecutive. A slow month doesn't reset progress; it simply doesn't count toward the 12.
- **Completion:** once the 12th qualified month is reached, DFY concludes, and the beneficiary receives the **Vetted Digital Systems Engineer (V. DsE.)** certification — a newly created credential, not a pre-existing industry standard.

**Worked example:** a beneficiary earns $2,400 in month 1 (qualified — 1 of 12, owes 20% = $480), $1,600 in month 2 (below threshold — doesn't advance the count, but still owes 20% = $320), $3,100 in month 3 (qualified — 2 of 12, owes 20% = $620), and so on. Every earning month generates a remittance regardless of qualification status; only qualified months move the counter toward 12.

**Why it's built this way, not just how:** a sub-threshold month costs real money and doesn't buy any progress toward finishing DFY — there's no such thing as a "free pass" or a coasting month. That's deliberate: it builds urgency and consistency pressure into the DSE period specifically because those are the same muscles SUC demands later. A beneficiary who learns to avoid slow months here is being prepared for SUC, not just taxed during DSE.

## What this structure resembles, worth having on record

DFY's shape — income-contingent payment, triggered only once a real earnings threshold is crossed — closely resembles a real, named financial instrument: an income share agreement (ISA). ISAs draw real regulatory attention in some jurisdictions (effective-APR disclosure, payment caps, treatment under consumer lending law) precisely because they look informal but function as a real financial obligation. That's not a reason to avoid the structure — it's a reason to get the same kind of real legal/financial review here that the SUC ownership policy already got, before this touches a real student's real payments.

## What's explicitly not covered here

BEF is separately developing a business-funding initiative for SUC-stage businesses. No structure, plan, or name exists yet — don't describe or invent mechanics for it. If asked, say plainly that it's in development and unspecified.
