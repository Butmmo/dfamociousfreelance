# The Start-up Catalyst

This is Stage 2 of DBI (D'Famocious Business Incubator, a subsidiary of D'Famocious Group) — the Start-up Catalyst (SUC) arm. Stage 1 is Digital Systems Engineering (DSE): the seven paths (Ascent, CareBridge, Ministry Systems, Revenue Recovery, SMB Optimization, Broadcast Engine, Authority Engine) — service-based, client-acquisition systems a student runs to build cash flow, proof, and expertise. Advancement to SUC is outcome-based, not simply time-based: a beneficiary becomes eligible for a SUC invitation once DSE has produced $450,000 in cumulative earnings within 3-5 years — or an equivalent amount over a longer timeframe, calculated at $90,000/year continuously from year 5 onward, so a slower but sustained pace still qualifies (`digital-systems-engineering`'s `references/pricing-and-dfy.md` has the full tiered calculator) — see "Ownership" below for the full pricing ladder, including direct entry without going through DSE first. **The Start-up Catalyst is the "how" for what comes next: turning that proof into an owned business — digital or traditional.** Fourteen paths exist for turning that proof into an owned business — seven Compounder products, seven Proprietor businesses (see "Choosing a track" below); this skill is the systematic path from choosing one to maturing it into a real, valuable, differentiated business.

**Working reference: SUC Constitution (Restated).** This skill implements and elaborates that document operationally; where the two differ, the Constitution governs.

The intended student has already spent real time monetizing tech through one of the seven DSE paths — typically the years it takes to cross $450,000 in cumulative earnings — and is financially literate, can read sales/performance data, and doesn't need beginner-level hand-holding.

## Choosing a track: fourteen paths, narrowed in two steps

Every Digital Engineering graduate enters Start-up Catalyst facing fourteen possible paths — seven Compounder products, seven Proprietor businesses. Getting to one final choice happens in two clean steps, not one leap.

- **The Compounder** builds something that grows in value without proportional added labor — a digital product, valued on retention and recurring revenue. Default guidance for this track to tech-weighted (Bubble, Lovable, Base44, Replit), but stay flexible — not every one of the seven is software.
- **The Proprietor** builds something real and stakes a name on it being trustworthy — a traditional business, valued on owned earnings and, in developing settings, on verification. Not tech-weighted — don't force SaaS logic onto this track.

Neither track is the default, the fallback, or "the real DBI path." Both are complete, equally developed, and equally viable — the difference between them is shape, not seriousness.

### Step 1 — Fourteen down to seven: pick the track

Three honest questions settle this quickly, and none of them is loaded toward either answer:

1. **Daily work** — building and refining something remotely, or being physically present in one place and its community?
2. **Where the edge comes from** — differentiation and content in a crowded digital market, or verification and trust in an often-informal local one?
3. **What Stage 1 already points toward** — check the "connects to" column in both `references/design-and-structure.md` and `references/traditional-business-types.md`. This is the strongest of the three signals, since it's proof the grad already generated, not a guess about temperament.

### Step 2 — Seven down to one: pick the specific path

Inside the chosen track, use the same logic again: steer toward whichever of the seven already matches the raw material — data, scripts, or relationship trust — the grad's Stage 1 path generated. That mapping is spelled out in full in each track's reference file. If nothing stands out from Stage 1 alone, don't guess — run stage 1 (Design) below and let a JTBD/switch-interview pass decide it instead.

Full detail on the Compounder's seven: `references/design-and-structure.md`. Full detail on the Proprietor's seven, across developed/developing/international settings: `references/traditional-business-types.md`.

That last row is worth remembering on its own: luxury access isn't uniform within either track — it's decided type by type, by whether the sale is relationship-brokered or structurally scarce (real luxury is reachable) or platform-compared and borrowed (it isn't), regardless of digital or traditional. Both tracks are now fully researched for this stage, product-by-product and type-by-type, with real case studies behind every ceiling claim in both fork files — including one correction along the way (Franchise/Acquisition was initially guessed as luxury-plausible on the territorial-exclusivity logic; closer research showed that logic doesn't hold, and the guess was fixed rather than left standing). That correction is itself the reason to trust the rest of this table: every claim in it has actually been checked against a real case, not just pattern-matched from the one that came first.

Track selection is **stage 0** — resolve it before stage 1, not alongside it. The ten stages below are written to apply to either track in general terms; where the mechanics genuinely diverge, the table above is the reference to check, not an assumption to default past.

## Ownership: what "yours" actually means by default

Getting in carries a real price attached to the equity terms, not just a flat entry fee: **$2,000 via the DSE path** (25% DBI equity — requires the $450,000/3-5-year threshold above and an invitation), **$6,000 entering SUC directly** (same 25% equity, paying to skip the proof DSE would have generated), or **$24,000 entering directly with DBI's equity reduced to 10%**. That third tier deliberately replaced an earlier no-equity option — a ladder that keeps DBI genuinely aligned with the business at every tier beats a cliff that removes its stake entirely at the top. Full reasoning behind these numbers, including why no clean external market comparable exists for pricing a not-yet-formed equity stake: the `digital-systems-engineering` skill's `references/pricing-and-dfy.md`.

**No rogue exits.** A founder can't sell controlling interest, unilaterally dissolve the business, exit entirely, or transfer core assets without DBI's explicit, documented, prior approval — this isn't a bureaucratic hurdle, it's what makes DBI's equity stake real rather than nominal; equity that a founder can walk away from unilaterally isn't actually equity. Pair this with a **fidelity clause** at formation: the founder commits to long-term financial, operational, and ethical alignment with DBI's structure for the life of the business, not just at signing.

**No SUC-stage tracking mechanism exists yet to apply this to, but the standard is already set:** any future SUC progress-tracking or mentorship system should run on the BPS Cadence (`dfg`'s `references/bps-cadence.md`) — daily binary tracking, weekly reporting, 40-day Belief/Affirmation/Evaluation cycles — for consistency with how DSE already operates, rather than inventing a separate rhythm when one eventually gets built.

**Confirmed, not just flagged:** BEF's constitution describes a percentage of a SUC business's Annual Net Profit remitted to DFG — this is the seminal-stage precursor to the current 25%/10% equity structure, not a separate or additional obligation. The specific numbers built here supersede the constitution's unspecified percentage; there's no double-counting.

**Recognition, not just obligation:** the SUC founder with the highest audited net profit by year's end is named D'Famocious Innovator of the Year — a real platform (conference speaking, a scoped one-year Elder Council seat), not just a title. Full detail: `digital-systems-engineering`'s `references/annual-recognition.md`.

Every business built through DBI — Compounder or Proprietor — defaults to D'Famocious Group holding 25% equity (range 20-30%, justified by training/methodology/brand, not capital), with the student holding the rest and running the business day to day. This stake is meant to step down at defined self-sufficiency milestones, or the student can buy out early through DBI's existing video-inquiry process; DBI's terms and conditions govern the specifics.

Two real exceptions to the default, and they're justified differently on purpose — the default 25% is earned by training and methodology, which every from-scratch build draws on regardless of who funds day-to-day operations; the exceptions below are earned by capital alone, because in both cases the thing being valued didn't come from DBI's training in the first place. Self-funding a new build (Retail, Real Estate, a Micro-SaaS tool, any of the fourteen) never exempts it from the default — only these two do, and only for the reason stated:

- **License product type:** the student stays licensor of record — DBI's 25% sits underneath as equity, it doesn't take over the licensing relationship itself. That relationship is the student's personal trust and track record; moving it to DBI would break the thing that makes it sellable.
- **Franchise/Business Acquisition:** only DBI-financed acquisitions default into this structure. A student who buys an existing business with their own capital owes DBI no equity in that specific deal — DBI contributed nothing to it. If DBI does finance or arrange the acquisition, equity scales to what was actually contributed, not the flat 25% — as a working default, use DBI's contribution as a simple share of the total purchase price (DBI puts in $10,000 of a $100,000 purchase, DBI holds roughly 10%). This is a simplification: real acquisition financing often prices equity against post-deal company value rather than raw contribution, which can land on a different number for larger or leveraged deals — treat the simple ratio as the starting default, not the final word, and flag that a more precise calculation may be warranted as deal size grows.

Brand disclosure to the business's own customers isn't required by default — the parent relationship is a cap-table fact, not a brand-identity one, and genuine independent (including luxury) positioning survives this fine. Check local requirements before assuming this holds everywhere; some jurisdictions require disclosure in specific formal contexts.

This is a working policy, not a substitute for real legal counsel — treat it as the default to operate from, not a finalized legal position, and get actual counsel before any equity or licensing paperwork is signed. One more consistency note: the 25% applies per subsidiary, not capped across a student's portfolio — a student who builds a Template Vault and later a Micro-SaaS tool has DBI holding 25% of each separately, since each is a distinct legal entity drawing on DBI's training and methodology in its own right, not one shared claim across everything the student ever builds.

## The sequence

Ten stages. Each has a one-line job and a reference file for depth.

1. **Design** — Find the job the product is really hired for (functional/emotional/social), not just the feature list. → `references/design-and-structure.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-design-and-validation.md` (Proprietor: feasibility study, starting capital by type)
2. **Structure** — Build the offer as a deliberate rung on a price ladder, not a standalone price. → `references/design-and-structure.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-structure-and-operations.md` (Proprietor: Good-Better-Best tiering)
3. **Optimize** — Get the build solid enough to handle real usage without breaking; what this actually means differs sharply by track (AI-build hardening for the Compounder, operational/verification systemizing for the Proprietor — see `references/proprietor-structure-and-operations.md`). → `references/build-and-test.md`
4. **Test** — Measure real fit before scaling spend: the 40% test and retention curve for tech products, a real-payment commitment test for everything else. → `references/build-and-test.md`
5. **Presence & pre-sell** — Build audience in public; take pre-orders on a tiered, descending discount tied to a real (not fake) scarcity constraint. → `references/presence-and-launch.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-presence-and-launch.md` (Proprietor: GBP/review generation, soft-then-grand opening)
6. **Launch** — Timeline and format follow product type; there is no one universal launch day. → `references/presence-and-launch.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-presence-and-launch.md` (Proprietor)
7. **Content & authority** — Founder-led, repurposed, niche-first content is the default engine here — same channel as stage 5, sustained past launch. → `references/presence-and-launch.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-presence-and-launch.md` (Proprietor)
8. **Ecosystem relevance** — Own a narrow niche completely before attempting to own a category; the second move needs resources the first doesn't. → `references/ecosystem-and-authority.md`
9. **Valuation** — Teach the real mechanics, routed by product shape: SaaS-style products use ARR multiples and retention metrics; everything else uses earnings multiples. → `references/valuation-saas.md` or `references/valuation-sde-and-licensing.md`
10. **The fork** — Every product eventually chooses a lane: stay comparable, go premium, or (rarely, and only for some product types) go luxury. → `references/the-fork.md` (Compounder) / `references/proprietor-the-fork.md` (Proprietor)

## The one rule that overrides everything else

Stages 5-9 run on growth-startup logic: discount to convert early buyers, be radically transparent in public, invite comparison to prove you're better. True luxury positioning (stage 10's rarest lane) runs on the **opposite** logic: no discounts, no comparison, restricted access on purpose. A product cannot do both at once. Before advising any pricing or content move for a product that's attempting the luxury lane, check whether it has formally exited the discount/transparency phase — see `references/the-fork.md` for the full doctrine and the inversion-point checklist. When in doubt, default to premium, not luxury; premium is achievable by any of the seven product types, luxury structurally is not.
