# Proprietor: Design & Validation

Covers stage 1 (Design) for the Proprietor track specifically — the "ideation" stage. This is the Proprietor's counterpart to the Compounder's JTBD/switch-interview work in `design-and-structure.md`; that lens still applies (find the functional/emotional/social job, including the trust job informal competitors fail at), but a traditional business needs a harder, capital-aware validation pass before committing, which is what this file adds.

## The feasibility study: go/no-go before a business plan

A feasibility study is not a business plan. A business plan assumes the answer is "go" and focuses on execution. A feasibility study is the decision itself — it exists to produce an honest go/no-go, including "no," before real capital moves. Run one before writing anything that assumes success.

Five parts, sized to the business, not padded for their own sake:

1. **Market feasibility** — Is there real, reachable demand? Use the seven types' demographic breakdowns in `traditional-business-types.md` as the starting segment list, then size it: Total Addressable Market (everyone who could ever want this), Small/Serviceable Available Market (who this specific business can actually reach — usually local, usually narrower than TAM by an order of magnitude or more), Serviceable Obtainable Market (realistic capture in year one). For a Proprietor business, SAM is almost always the binding constraint — a home-service business in one city has a real ceiling no amount of marketing changes.
2. **Technical/operational feasibility** — Can this actually be delivered at the quality bar promised? For a Proprietor business this usually means: licensing/certification requirements, supplier or contractor relationships, physical space or equipment, and staffing — not a build-vs-buy software question.
3. **Financial feasibility** — Does the math work at realistic (not optimistic) volume? See starting capital below; pair it with the SDE math in `valuation-sde-and-licensing.md` to sanity-check what a realistic year-one and year-three P&L actually looks like.
4. **Organizational/legal feasibility** — Licensing, permits, business registration, insurance, and (in a developing setting) the formalization question raised in `traditional-business-types.md`'s Franchise/Acquisition section.
5. **Go/no-go recommendation** — State it plainly. A feasibility study that doesn't produce a clear answer wasn't worth running.

## Starting capital by type (directional; US figures except where noted)

Order-of-magnitude, not a quote — real numbers vary by state/city, format, and whether the grad enters solo or builds a full operation from day one.

| Type | Low-capital entry | Fuller operation |
|---|---|---|
| Systemized Local Service | $2,000-$10,000 (solo, equipment-light) | $10,000-$25,000+ |
| Retail & Consumer Trading | $500-$5,000 (social-commerce/dropship model — no upfront inventory, sell via TikTok Shop/WhatsApp/Instagram first, fulfill only after real orders land) | $50,000-$150,000+ (inventory, fixtures, lease deposit, build-out) |
| Real Estate & Property | $2,000-$32,000 (solo licensed agent under a broker) | $50,000-$150,000+ (own brokerage: office, multi-agent licensing, marketing) |
| Food, Beverage & Hospitality | $50,000-$150,000 (food truck / ghost kitchen) | $150,000-$750,000+ (full-service restaurant, format-dependent) |
| Agro-Processing & Light Manufacturing | Nigeria: locally-fabricated/compact equipment, low and variable — start small on affordable local machinery and scale with profit. US: use a contract co-packer instead of owning equipment at all | Nigeria: $200,000-$400,000 for a mid-scale, export-capable plant (equipment, civil works, working capital, compliance). US: $150,000-$2,500,000+ for an owned small-batch facility, category-dependent |
| Franchise or Business Acquisition | Under $100,000 (home-based/service brands are the fastest-growing entry tier) | $100,000-$750,000+ is where most buyers actually land; $750,000+ for full-service/high-infrastructure brands |
| Import/Export & Cross-Border Trade | $5,000-$25,000 (lean, home-based, single product line) | $100,000-$350,000+ (warehousing, broader product range, in-house logistics) |

All seven types now have real, sourced capital ranges — no remaining gaps in this table.

One pattern worth teaching directly: **every type in this table has a real low-capital entry point that isn't the only way in, but is a legitimate way to prove the concept before committing to the fuller operation** — a solo real estate agent before a brokerage, a food truck before a restaurant, a home-based franchise before a build-out-heavy one. Route a capital-constrained grad toward that entry tier rather than the ceiling figure; it's a smaller, faster feasibility test in its own right, not a lesser ambition.
