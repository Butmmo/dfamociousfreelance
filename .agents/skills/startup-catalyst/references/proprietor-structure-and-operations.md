# Proprietor: Structure & Operations

Covers stages 2 (Structure) and 3 (Optimize) for the Proprietor track. Structure is pricing/packaging a single business into tiers; Optimize is what "systemize the operational/verification layer" (named in `SKILL.md`'s comparison table) actually means in practice.

## Structure: Good-Better-Best, built for packageable work

Good-Better-Best (Bronze/Silver/Gold) tiering is the direct Proprietor equivalent of the Compounder's value ladder — but it operates *within* one business rather than *across* seven products. A lawn care example shows the shape: a basic tier (mowing, trimming, cleanup), a standard tier (basic plus edging and fertilizing), and a deluxe tier (standard plus seasonal extras) — three clearly different, clearly escalating packages, not the same job at three prices.

**Where it works:** routine, repeatable services that package together cleanly — cleaning, lawn care, maintenance, most of Type 1 and parts of Type 4. **Where it doesn't:** one-off, highly custom work (a single real estate transaction, a bespoke development project) — GBB needs enough repetition to make tiers meaningful, so route those types toward package-by-scope (a defined project fee structure) instead of GBB.

Two mechanics worth building in deliberately:
- **Make the middle tier the visible default.** Most buyers anchor against it rather than evaluating three options independently — a "Most Popular" label on the middle tier is doing real work, not just decoration.
- **Use tiering to price by value, not by the clock.** Time-based pricing punishes efficiency (the faster you work, the less you earn); package-based pricing lets a well-systemized operator earn more per hour precisely *because* they're systemized, without the customer feeling penalized for it.

## Optimize: what "systemize the operational/verification layer" actually means

Not the Compounder's AI-build loop — the Proprietor's optimize pass is about documentation and consistency, using two connected tools:

1. **Map the journey, then write it down.** Trace what actually happens from first contact through payment and follow-up (a simple version: Sales → Fulfillment → Invoicing → Payment → Retention). Anywhere the business does something the same way every time, that's a Standard Operating Procedure waiting to be documented — write it as clear, step-by-step instructions a new hire (or the owner on a bad day) could follow without guessing.
2. **Build quality checkpoints with specific, objective criteria — not "check for quality."** A checkpoint that just says "check for quality" gives no real guidance and produces inconsistent results depending on who's checking. A checkpoint that asks concrete yes/no questions — did the technician arrive within the window, was the before/after photo taken, does the invoice match the quote — is checkable by anyone and produces the same answer regardless of who's asking.

**Why this matters more here than it would for a Compounder product:** in a developing-country setting specifically, this SOP-and-checklist layer *is* the cross-cutting trust-infrastructure differentiator named in `SKILL.md` — the documented, checkable process is what a systemized operator has that an informal competitor doesn't. Optimize and the trust-infrastructure rule aren't two separate ideas; the SOP layer is the concrete mechanism that rule runs on.

One proportionality warning worth passing on directly: checkpoints that take too long relative to the stakes get skipped. A two-hour job doesn't need a thirty-minute review. Match the checklist's weight to what's actually at risk, or the system quietly stops being used.
