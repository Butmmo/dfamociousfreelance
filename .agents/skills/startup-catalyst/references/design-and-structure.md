# Design & Structure

Covers stages 1-2 of The Start-up Catalyst.

## The seven product types (the "what")

| # | Product | Typical price | Year-readiness |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Template & Asset Vault | $27-147 | Year 1+ |
| 2 | Niche Intelligence / Data Product | $47-497 | Year 1+ |
| 3 | Signature Course | $197-997 self-paced, $997-2,997 cohort | Year 1-2+ |
| 4 | Membership / Continuity Community | $29-79/mo | Year 1-2+ |
| 5 | Micro-SaaS / AI Tool | $19-199/mo per seat | Year 2-3+ |
| 6 | High-Ticket Mastermind / Certification | $1,500-10,000+ | Year 3+ |
| 7 | Business-in-a-Box License | $997-5,000, sometimes + revenue share | Year 3+ |

These prices are not independent data points — laid end to end, they already form a value ladder (see below). A student building product #5 (Micro-SaaS) doesn't need to abandon product #1 (Template Vault); the Vault can be the ladder's entry rung that earns trust for the SaaS tool later.

## Which Stage 1 path pairs naturally with which product

| Stage 1 path | Natural fits | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The Ascent | Template Vault, Course, Mastermind/Certification | Sales closing is human-delivered IP — scripts and frameworks teach well, but don't automate into software |
| CareBridge | Data Product, Micro-SaaS, License | The mystery-shop audit data is resellable research; the Zapier automation is already SaaS-shaped; the system is franchise-ready |
| Ministry Systems | Mastermind/Certification, Course, Membership | A trust-sensitive niche (churches) rewards a credential most; relationship-first sales resists automation |
| Revenue Recovery Engine | Template Vault, Data Product, Micro-SaaS | Klaviyo flow exports are literal template material; store-scoring data and the audit workflow both productize directly |
| SMB Optimization System | Template Vault, License, Course | GHL snapshots are template material; this is DBI's most-proven, most franchise-ready path |
| Broadcast Engine | Data Product, Course, Membership | Genre/YouTube-gap analysis is resellable research; production skill teaches well |
| Authority Engine | Data Product, Course, Micro-SaaS | ICP research productizes directly; the targeting logic can become a lightweight tool |

Not exhaustive or exclusive — any grad can attempt any of the seven products. But the natural fit means less redesign work, because the Stage 1 path already generated exactly the raw material (scored data, scripts, or relationship trust) that product type needs. Steer toward the natural fit first; treat an off-fit choice as needing more upfront design work, not as a bad idea.

## Design: find the job, not just the feature

Every product gets "hired" for a job with three layers:

- **Functional** — the task it performs
- **Emotional** — how the buyer wants to feel using it
- **Social** — how the buyer wants to be perceived for having it

A product that only wins on the functional layer ends up competing on price and features alone, because that's the one axis every competitor can also claim. This is precisely why the Apple Watch Edition failed and the Apple Watch Ultra succeeded — see `the-fork.md` for the full case study. The lesson for Design: before building, be explicit about which emotional and social job the product is doing that a cheaper or more generic alternative can't.

**Practical tool: the switch interview.** Talk to 10-15 people about the specific moment they switched to (or away from) a comparable product — not a generic "what do you want" survey, but the actual story of the switch. This is reported to meaningfully reshape roadmaps and lift conversion when done properly before a product is finalized. Run this before stage 3 (Optimize), not after.

## Structure: the offer as a ladder rung, not a standalone price

A value ladder sequences offers from low-price/wide-reach at the bottom to high-price/narrow-audience at the top. Each rung's real job is to build enough trust and proof that the next rung becomes an easy yes — a jump from a $200 entry offer to a $20,000 engagement isn't a "100x ask" if it's the obvious next step for someone who already got a result at the bottom.

Two rules to hold the ladder together:

1. **Each rung must deliver a meaningfully different result, not just more content or access.** A $500 version of a $50 product that's just "more of the same" collapses the ladder — buyers stop trusting that the next rung is worth the jump.
2. **Within any single product, use Good-Better-Best tiering** rather than a single price point. A visible middle tier does real work: it's commonly the one that captures the most buyers (anchored against the "Best" tier, cheaper than going all-in, more than the base) and it's a legitimate lever for reducing churn and lifting upgrade rate on subscription products specifically.

## Structuring for the product's actual valuation shape

How a product is packaged should anticipate how it will eventually be valued (see `valuation-saas.md` and `valuation-sde-and-licensing.md`):

- **SaaS-shaped products** (Micro-SaaS, subscription Data Product) should be packaged on the subscription-to-usage spectrum from day one — tiered plans or usage-based pricing, not one-off sales — because recurring revenue is what the eventual valuation multiple actually rewards.
- **Everything else** (Course, Membership, Mastermind, License) should favor recurring or repeat-purchase components wherever legitimately possible (a membership tier attached to a course, a renewal fee attached to a license) — earnings-multiple valuation consistently rewards recurring revenue with a premium over one-off sales, even inside the same business.

Structure the pricing architecture around this from the start; retrofitting recurring revenue onto a product built as a one-off sale is much harder than designing it in.
